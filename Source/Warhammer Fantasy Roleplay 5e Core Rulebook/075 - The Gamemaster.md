# <span id="page-260-0" data-folio="261"></span>• **THE GAMEMASTER** •

Bringing the world of **Warhammer Fantasy Roleplay** to life for your friends is a richly rewarding experience. If you have never run a roleplaying game before, it might seem a little intimidating, but with a few tricks of the trade, you'll find it easy and fun.

The most important rule in a running roleplaying game is to ensure that everyone at the table, you included, has fun. This chapter will set out your role as the GM, and provide you with the tools you need to do it well, while having a good time. Let's start with what exactly the GM does.

- You Tell the Story
- You Decide How the World Reacts
- You Decide When the Rules Are Needed
- You Arbitrate the Rules

# **You Tell the Story**

As a GM, you narrate the events of the story your players embark on, and portray every non-player character (NPC) they encounter. You play the role of the ruthless tyrant demanding satisfaction for a petty grievance and the pathetic urchin seeking just a penny or two for your ailing sibling. You describe the way the wind whispers through the trees, bringing the howls of distant wolves to those with ears keen enough to discern them. Every sense is yours to convey the world to your players: sights, sounds, smells, tastes, and touch. Your descriptions are the foundations upon which all that occurs in your game will be built. You do not always have to be lavish with them, but strive to have one or two memorable scenes in every game.

#### **You Decide How the World Reacts**

Roleplaying games differ from traditional storytelling in that your players get to decide what their Characters do and say. When this happens, you get to decide how the NPCs around them respond. If the Witch Hunter decides to throw their authority around with the local graf, you're the one who chooses the graf 's reaction — possibly with the help of some dice rolls (see our next point, below). When the party foils the cult's plans and sets the watch on its leader, you tell your players what happens next: how the authorities deal with the heretic, and how the rest of the cult, still in hiding, responds to the players' actions.

In this way, you keep the story moving forward, adapting to the decisions your players make along the way. You'll usually have an idea of how the story should end, but the exact details will in large part be decided by these reactions.

# **You Decide When the Rules Are Needed**

If you read **Chapter 5**, you'll know that we've provided rules for a wide variety of eventualities. But a lot of the time, you won't need to use these rules to play the game. Very often when your players decide to do something, it just happens without any need to roll dice. This is especially true for conversations, which make up a lot of roleplaying games. When the Investigator is questioning witnesses, they will often simply tell what they know, as long as the player has chosen the right people to talk to and the correct questions to ask. Your player will only need to roll a Charm Test (or Intimidate, depending on their style of questioning) if the witness has some reason to resist.

A large part of being the GM is deciding when to bring in the rules to decide the outcome of an action, and when to just let the story continue and keep the game flowing. Which brings us to our next point.

# **FAILING FORWARD**

One common mistake first-time GMs make is asking a player to roll a Test when failing that Test will stop the adventure in its tracks. If the party needs to get past that door to find the cult's lair, don't make them roll to unlock it unless you have a plan for what happens if they fail — perhaps a cultist hears them fiddling with the lock and opens it to see what's happening, getting the party inside but losing the element of surprise. If the vital next clue to the mystery is hidden in a dusty library, only call for a Test to determine how quickly it is uncovered, not *if* it is.

Every time you call for a Test, have a plan for both success and failure. Failure should move the story on, but with some negative consequences.

#### <span id="page-261-0" data-folio="262"></span>**You Arbitrate the Rules**

The rules exist as a framework to decide how actions play out in a fair and transparent way. The chance for failure is what keeps a game interesting and makes success all the sweeter. What the rules shouldn't do is get in the way of having fun.

Now and then, situations will crop up in your games where the rules don't quite apply to exactly what is happening — we've given Strength characteristics for a selection of inanimate objects, but even we couldn't predict your Flagellant would wind up Entangled by the large tub of porridge they fell into. At times like this, your job is to decide the Strength of porridge on the fly, and keep the game moving forward.

Similarly, two players might disagree on how they interpret some aspect of the rules, or even with how you interpret it. In these cases, the GM serves as the final word on how the interpretation should be decided, at least for now, so the game can continue.

This doesn't mean you rule your players with an iron fist, enforcing your way or the highway. Simply that your role is to come to a decision quickly, so the game doesn't get bogged down in arguing over rules, and everyone can get back to having fun foiling the plots of the Ruinous Powers.

There is further sound advice to be found on the running of roleplaying games on Cubicle 7's website and literally tens of thousands of hours more online from sources too numerous to list; however, the rest of this chapter is devoted specifically to the running of this game before you.
